My research investigates how firms embedded in transnational supply chains navigate and shape global climate governance and other foreign policies. By integrating insights from political science, economics, and corporate studies, I develop a new framework for understanding global trade politics that emphasizes how firms' regulatory exposure across supply chains generates distinct political preferences and coalitions. This approach uncovers new cleavages in corporate lobbying around climate regulations, sanctions, and industrial policy. My dissertation explores three core questions: (1) how supply-chain exposure across different regulatory contexts drives corporate political preferences over transnational carbon regulations, (2) how firm-level interdependence shapes corporate responses to sanctions, and (3) how asset exposure informs corporate climate policy engagement. My other research projects examine how firms reconfigure supplier networks under new climate rules, spread environmental norms and policies to emerging economies, and manage regulatory risks in authoritarian markets. My research utilizes large-scale observational data on firms, their supply chain characteristics, and their political activities. I have also developed a novel text analysis pipeline with computational methods to infer political stances. 

Additionally, I am interested in other issues related to de-globalization. A co-authored paper with Professor Enze Han on Chinese public backlash against international migration has been published in the Journal of Contemporary China.

## Global Value Chains and Corporate Opposition to Sanctions

Abstract

Economic sanctions entail high costs not only for target states but also for sending states, particularly their business sectors. While considerable literature exists on state-level factors that affect sanction design and enforcement, less is known about business actors' responses to and political influence on sanctions. This paper examines the circumstances under which firms attempt to influence sanction policies during the decision-making process of the state imposing them. Specifically, it investigates how firms' international connections affect their motivation to lobby against sanctions in the US Congress. I argue that firms will be more likely to lobby against sanctions on a particular state when the state plays a more important role in the firms' global value chains, as well as when the sanctions would harm the firms' foreign investments and trade. I test my argument using original data on US firms' lobbying over bills proposing economic sanctions against Russia from 2014 to 2022. The findings support my expectations. This paper is the first to explain, from the perspective of firms, how their global economic ties shape their stance on sanctions, offering an understanding of how private sector interests influence foreign policy decisions.

## Naturalized Athletes and Racialized National Identity in China (with Enze Han)


Abstract

This paper focuses on how the phenomenon of naturalized athletes has caused unprecedented domestic discussions on issues of national identity in China. Using online data collected from Sina Weibo, it discusses how the Chinese public reacted to prominent cases of naturalization of athletes in such different sports fields as men’s football and a variety of winter sports. It interrogates how Chinese citizens view the possibilities and realities of this particular category of immigrants, and how the appropriate boundaries of 'Chineseness' have been debated and contested. The paper found that Chinese public reaction to naturalization of foreign athletes is overall mixed, which is conditioned on an ethno-racial view of Chinese descent, cultural heritage and linguistic affinity. The paper concludes with reflections on the ethno-racial foundation of the Chinese national identity and the challenges the country will face in a future of accelerated international migration.


*Journal of Contemporary China*, 33(145), 52–64.[Link](https://doi.org/10.1080/10670564.2023.2179391)

## Do Firm-level Climate Change Exposures Drive Firm Political Actions in Climate Change? (with Xun Cao)


Abstract

Are firms with higher climate change physical and regulatory risks more likely to influence climate policies? Do firms with climate-related opportunities push for stronger climate policies? Drawing on newly available data, we examine how firm-level climate risks shape three political actions: lobbying, ad hoc coalition participation, and donations. Our analysis covers all listed firms in North America from 2001 to 2020. We find that firm physical exposure to climate change does not affect political actions, while regulatory and opportunity exposures both increase the likelihood of lobbying. Furthermore, regulatory exposure is positively linked to joining either pro- or anti-climate policy coalitions, whereas opportunity exposure is positively associated only with pro-climate coalitions. Finally, none of the climate exposure measures predicts firms’ political donations. Overall, these findings highlight the distinct ways in which firm-level climate change risks and opportunities drive political engagement in climate politics.

##  Embedded Global Supply Chains and Firms’ Political Strategies: The Case of the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism 

Abstract

This paper examines how transnational supply chains shape firms' political responses to climate-related trade policies. I argue that global supply chains generate asymmetric regulatory exposure: firms face different political incentives depending on the environmental regulations governing their upstream suppliers. Firms sourcing from highly regulated jurisdictions are more likely to support carbon tariffs, which reinforce their competitive advantage by imposing costs on less-regulated rivals. In contrast, firms embedded in supply chains linked to the least regulated jurisdictions are more likely to resist such policies, given the compliance costs they incur. To test the theory, I analyze the case of the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM). Using firm-level supply chain data and political activity records from the EU Transparency Register, I empirically analyze whether reliance on suppliers from states with different levels of emission regulatory stringency shapes EU-based firms' support or opposition to CBAM and related legislation. Text analysis techniques are also applied to firms' submitted documents during the EU's public consultation to determine their stances. The results show that variation in suppliers' regulatory environments systematically divides firms' positions on climate tariffs, underscoring how global supply chains produce new political cleavages in climate governance.

##  Have Global Supply Chains Made Developing Country Supplier Firms Greener? Evidence from Chinese Manufacturing Firms’ Carbon Emissions (with Xun Cao, Mingqin Wu)

Abstract

Do global supply chains help diffuse environmental norms to developing countries? Are firms in developing economies more likely to adopt greener practices when linked to foreign buyers in cleaner, more regulated markets? This paper contributes to debates on norm diffusion and private environmental governance by examining how transnational production ties affect supplier firms' carbon emissions in developing countries. Using a 2011-2015 Chinese firm panel data of over half a million observations, we adopt a difference-in-differences (DiD) design to estimate the causal effect of a supplier firm status on a firm's CO2 emissions. We find that becoming a supplier firm significantly reduces a firm’s carbon emission, by about 42.3\% in our main model specification. This main result holds in various robustness checks, when we use matched samples and a  post-stratification reweighted sample, and when we only include ever-treated firms in the analysis to control for potential selection bias.  

##  Do Carbon Tariffs Reshape Global Supply Chains? Examining the Effect of the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) on European Firms' Supply Chain Adjustments (with Xun Cao)

Abstract

Border carbon tariffs are a central component of emerging green industrial policies. But their capacity to reshape global production networks remains theoretically contested and empirically untested. Existing work focuses on aggregated trade flows and carbon leakage, leaving open a fundamental question: do firms restructure their supply chains in anticipation of regulatory costs, and through what mechanisms? This paper provides the first systematic firm-level test of how the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) reshapes global supply chains. We argue that anticipated CBAM regulatory exposure creates asymmetric incentives across existing supplier relationships by accelerating exit from carbon-intensive ties while selectively deepening links with cleaner production hubs. Further, this portfolio greening operates primarily through firms' own sourcing decisions rather than through upstream regulatory convergence. Using a novel panel dataset of 3,645 European customer firms and over 40,000 supply chain relationships from 2014 to 2024, we show that CBAM exposure leads European downstream customer firms to shift toward greener upstream portfolios even before formal implementation. Testing three specific mechanisms, we find that CBAM disproportionately raises the risk of supplier relationship termination, but significantly less so for suppliers in greener states. We find more modest but supporting evidence that CBAM increases the probability of forming new ties with cleaner suppliers. Notably, we find no evidence that this greening is driven by supplier countries ratcheting up their own climate regulations, suggesting that supply chain restructuring reflects buyer-side adaptation so far rather than a diffusion of climate regulations. These findings carry implications for how border carbon policies should be designed to achieve genuine decarbonization rather than supply chain displacement.


##  Weaponizing Ambiguity: How China's Strategic Regulatory Uncertainty Reshapes FDI in an Era of Geopolitical Tension

Abstract

What shapes the design of geopoliticized industrial policy? Powerful states deploy control over critical nodes in global production networks to impose costs on rivals. While the existing literature largely focus on either the state-level bargaining or firms' adaptation, less is known about a basic question of implementation: among the home-country firms that industrial policy is meant to mobilize, why some firms are pushed to absorb the costs of decoupling while others are accommodated by government support and compensations?  I argue that the answer depends on where a home country firm sits in the supply chains being restructured. Home country firms are not uniform targets of industrial policy; they differ in two key dimensions connecting them to the supply chain networks between the adversary and their home state. The first one is relational embeddedness on the upstream side: how deeply a home country firm is tied into specialized supplier-customer relationships with the upstream partner firms in adversary countries' deeper, longer-term, and more specialized ties create higher costs when governments force a break; these costs motivate resistance. However, the costs alone do not guarantee that states respond. The second dimension is bridge position to the further downstream market: whether partner occupies a critical place in the downstream production network when many downstream relationships run through it and alternative routes are limited. In those positions, trying to bypass this firm does not just hurt the firm itself; it creates delays and adjustment costs for further downstream domestic producers and customers. Therefore, states are most likely to accommodate the firms that are both hard to replace in their own relationships and hard to route around in the wider network.

Empirically, the paper examines variation among US manufacturing firms embedded in supply chains between China and the US from 2010 to 2023, using the Factset Revere Dataset to construct firm-level supply chain measures. The analysis links these measures to three outcomes: whether a US firm mobilizes politically, what kind of request it seeks, and what accommodation it receives –all three measured using data from the Senate Lobbying Disclosure Act database and Subsidy Tracker on federal subsidy records. The key explanatory variables are relational embeddedness, constructed from the depth and duration of the upstream Chinese supplier ties, and bridge position, constructed from the firm's centrality linking to the downstream US producers' or customers' network. Three preliminary findings emerge. First, firms high on both dimensions are more likely to lobby on geopoliticized industrial policy bills. Second, lobbying firms devote a larger share of their lobbying to compensation-oriented requests rather than outright opposition. Third, US firms high on both relational embeddedness and bridge position are also more likely to receive federal subsidy support.


##  IO-Linked Climate Regimes and the Supply Chain as a Regulatory Channel

Abstract

Climate regulation varies dramatically across jurisdictions, but global supply chains operate as integrated systems that span them all. Whether international climate governance can exploit this consistency to reach firms beyond any single state's authority is a question the existing literature has not posed. I argue that it can. Enrollment in an international organization (IO)-linked climate regime converts lead firms into relay governors, who translate institutional authority borrowed from the international regime into supplier-facing rules enforced through commercial leverage. Using a firm-year panel of United Nations Global Compact participants from 2015 to 2024, I test whether entry into the Science Based Targets initiative is associated with the formalization of supplier-facing climate governance, measured through supervised classification of Communications on Progress, participation in the CDP Supply Chain Program, and comprehensiveness of Scope 3 disclosure. Identification comes from staggered difference-in-differences with not-yet-treated controls. FactSet Revere supply chain data let me test whether buyer market share and supplier dependence condition the effect. The results illuminate how the commercial architecture of global production can serve as a conduit for international climate governance where jurisdictional authority cannot.

##  Adaptation Where Production Happens: How Supply Chains Drive Climate Resilience

Abstract

Hershey and other global chocolate manufacturers increasingly invest in farmer training, agroforestry, and other rural resilience initiatives in West Africa, where much of the world's cocoa is grown. These efforts are striking because climate adaptation in low-income regions is usually framed as a state- and donor-led response to local vulnerability. However, climate stress in Ghana and Cote d'Ivoire threatens not only rural livelihoods but also the supply chains on which major chocolate manufacturers depend. 

I argue that supply chains are an underappreciated channel through which adaptation is developed. Powerful downstream firms that source from climate-vulnerable local producers often have strong incentives to support resilience where production happens. When climate shocks threaten output in origins that are geographically concentrated and difficult to replace, downstream firms cannot easily secure supply through substitution alone. Instead, they increasingly take up adaptation functions themselves by supporting resilience measures. Supply chains therefore do not simply transmit climate risk but also shape local adaptation on the ground. To test this argument, I construct a firm-origin-year panel for 2015–2024 across several climate-sensitive agricultural supply chains, including cocoa, coffee, palm oil, and cotton. The analysis combines three types of data. First, I measure firm dependence on specific sourcing origins using Factset Supply Chain Data and firms’ public responsible-sourcing reports. Second, I capture climate exposure in supplier regions using temperature and rainfall anomalies from ERA5-Land and CHIRPS, matched to crop-specific growing periods using FAO crop calendars and spatial crop maps. Third, I collect original data of firm-supported adaptation as outcomes by coding sustainability reports, press releases, and recurring sectoral reporting platforms for observable resilience measures in supplier regions, including supplier training, adaptation finance, agroforestry support, and collaboration with public or nonprofit actors. 

The paper aims to shift attention from states and donors alone to the role of transnational production networks in organizing resilience. More broadly, it shows that adaptation is increasingly shaped by the needs of global supply chains: downstream customer firms are not merely reacting to climate risk, but helping determine where adaptation resources flow, which local actors receive support, and what kinds of resilience are ultimately built.
